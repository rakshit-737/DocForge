# Condition Survey of the Alder Street Reading Room

This report records the condition of the reading room at 14 Alder Street as found during three
inspection visits in the spring of 2026. It is written for the trustees, who must decide during
the coming year how to phase the repairs that the building now plainly needs, and it is arranged
so that a reader with no building background can follow the argument from evidence to costed
recommendation without leaving the document.

[^charter]: The reading room operates under a charter of 1911, held at the borough archive as
  item AC/1911/44. The charter binds the trustees to keep the building wind and water tight,
  which is the standard this survey applies throughout.

## Scope and method

The survey was commissioned in writing by the clerk to the trustees[^1] and carried out to the
level of an elevated visual inspection[^2]. No fabric was opened up, no drainage was rodded, and
nothing was tested in a laboratory; where those limits matter to a finding, a note says so at the
point where the reader will want it. Three visits were made in all[^visits], two in dry weather
and one, deliberately, during sustained rain.

Each element of the building was examined in a fixed order, outside before inside and top before
bottom, so that water was followed in the direction it travels. Findings are reported in that
same order. Where a defect is visible in more than one place, it is described once at its worst
occurrence and cross-referred in prose elsewhere, which keeps the schedule of repairs short
enough to act on.

The report separates three registers of statement: what was seen, what is inferred from what was
seen, and what is recommended. Seen and inferred are never mixed in one sentence. The trustees
asked for this discipline after the previous survey, and it has been kept even where it makes a
paragraph slower to read.

## Fabric of the ground floor

### The entrance hall

The entrance hall sets the tone of the building and, unhappily, of this report. The tiled floor
is sound, but the plaster of the west wall carries a tide mark of salt staining to roughly one
metre above the skirting, and the paint above it has blown in patches the size of a spread hand.
The staining is characteristic of moisture rising from the base of the wall rather than falling
from above[^damp], and the pattern has plainly developed over years rather than months.

The joinery of the entrance screen is in good order. Its lower rail shows old worm flight holes,
none active, and the draught lobby doors close against their stops without binding. The hall
ceiling is uncracked, and the cornice runs true along all four sides.

### The stack room

The stack room behind the hall holds the closed shelves and sees the fewest visitors, which is
why its problems have gone unreported the longest. Shelving bay G leans away from the wall by a
finger's width at its head; the fixing plugs have failed in soft plaster rather than reaching
brick. Books have been removed from its top two shelves as a precaution, and the bay should not
be reloaded until it is refixed.

Ventilation in this room is poor. The single sash is painted shut, the two air bricks in the
external wall are blocked with debris, and on the wet-weather visit the room smelled distinctly
of damp paper. None of the stock shows mould, but conditions in this room are drifting toward
the range in which mould becomes likely, and the remedy is cheap if taken early.

## The first floor

The reading room proper occupies the whole first floor, lit by six tall sashes and warmed, as it
has been since 1934, by two cast iron column radiators. The floor deflects perceptibly under a
firm step near the centre of the span. Deflection alone is not alarm; timber floors of this span
and age move. What matters is whether the movement is historic or live, and the evidence here
points to historic[^joists], though the point deserves confirmation from below when the ground
floor ceiling is next opened.

The sashes are the best feature of the building and the worst maintained. Four of the six run
freely; two are wedged with folded card, their cords broken. Glazing throughout is the original
drawn glass, with its slight rolling distortion, and it should be kept[^glazing]. Putty is
failing along the bottom rails of the three sashes on the weather side, and one pane in the
south west light carries a diagonal crack held by old tape.

The plaster ceiling shows a single long crack running east to west along the line of a known
beam. The crack is stable in width along its whole run, its edges are dirty rather than fresh,
and no debris was found on the shelving beneath. Taken together these signs point to an old and
settled movement, not a current one.

## Roof, parapets and rainwater goods

The roof was inspected from a tower scaffold at two corners and by camera elsewhere. The slates
are of two ages, the larger area original and a patch of perhaps forty slates renewed after the
1987 storm. Slippage is confined to the older area, where fixing nails have corroded; eleven
slates are displaced and two are missing outright. The leadwork of the parapet gutters is
nearing the end of its life[^lead], and it is the leadwork, not the slates, that should set the
timetable for the roof contract.

The parapets themselves stand plumb, but their coping joints are open and the mortar of the top
three courses is soft enough to rake out by hand. Open joints here feed water directly into the
wall head, and the staining on the reading room ceiling below the south east corner is the
indoor signature of exactly this path.

Rainwater goods are cast iron and complete, which is rare and worth keeping. Two rear downpipes
are blocked at their heads and overflow at the offset in heavy rain; the wet-weather visit
caught both in the act, sheeting water down the face of the brickwork. Clearing them is a
half-day's work and would do more for the building per pound spent than any other single item in
this report.

## Matters held at low confidence

Not every finding in a survey deserves the same weight, and it would be a disservice to the
trustees to print guesses in the same voice as observations. *The eastern chimney was not opened
and was not smoke tested, and every statement in this report that concerns its flue should be
read as provisional until a sweep's camera has been through it[^flue].* The same reservation
applies, with less force, to the drainage runs, which were observed working in rain but never
traced.

The cellar was inspected only from the ladder head, since the fixed ladder itself failed a hand
test at its upper bolts. What could be seen was dry, and the brick vault showed no distress, but
the space below the stack room floor could not be sighted at all and is the report's largest
blind spot.

## Recommendations and programme

The repairs divide naturally into three phases. First, within the month: clear the two blocked
downpipe heads, refix shelving bay G, and free the painted sash in the stack room. Second,
within the year: the roof contract, taking the parapet copings and the gutter leadwork together,
since the scaffold that serves one serves the other. Third, within three years: the ground floor
plaster, which cannot usefully be renewed until a season has passed after the external works and
the wall base has begun to dry.

Costs are stated in the covering letter rather than in this report, at the clerk's request, and
are built up from measured quantities rather than allowances[^costs]. The phasing above keeps
each year's outlay inside the figure the trustees indicated at commissioning.

Nothing in this report should alarm the trustees, and one thing should encourage them: the
building's defects are the cheap, patient kind that reward early attention. The charter's
standard of wind and water tight[^charter] is within reach in a single programme year, and the
reading room will then be the better part of a century away from needing another report of this
length.

[^1]: Letter of engagement dated 12 January 2026, reference AT/26/07, countersigned by two
  trustees as the standing orders require.
[^2]: As defined in the practice guidance for surveys of heritage buildings, third edition,
  section 4: accessible surfaces viewed from ladders and tower scaffolds, no opening up.
[^visits]: 3 February in dry frost, 18 February in dry mild weather, and 9 March during the
  sustained rain that the method statement required for the rainwater goods.
[^damp]: The tide mark form, with salts concentrated at the top of the stain, distinguishes
  moisture rising through the wall base from a plumbing leak or a driving rain failure, both of
  which stain from a point and spread downward or radially. The distinction matters because the
  remedies differ by an order of magnitude in cost, and because the cheaper remedy, improved
  ground drainage and freer evaporation, is defeated rather than helped by the impermeable
  renders that are commonly sold for the purpose. This note is deliberately the longest in the
  report because the finding it supports carries the largest sum in phase three.
[^joists]: The deflection is even, centred on mid-span, and unchanged between the February and
  March visits under the same test load, namely the same surveyor.
[^glazing]: Drawn glass of this period survives in fewer than one building in ten of this age
  locally, and replacement float glass reads as flat and dead beside it.
[^lead]: The gutters show fatigue cracking at the drips and two amateur patches in bituminous
  tape. Lead of this profile typically serves eighty to a hundred years; these linings are at
  ninety two.
[^flue]: The chimney serves the disused boiler only, so the reservation carries no safety
  weight while the flue remains capped; it would gain weight at once if the trustees revive the
  heating scheme.
[^costs]: Quantities were taken on site by the surveyor and priced against the borough's
  measured term contract rates for 2026, which the clerk holds.
